MODE · one brand · three marks

Just MODE. Three geometric marks.

No modes, no system: one name, drawn three different ways. Each follows a different logic, from the same tradition as Fletcher's Design Objectives and Hollenstein's L'Echoppe: a few pure shapes, one idea, nothing extra.

Option 1 · after Alan Fletcher

Two Shapes

MODE is two shapes, each used twice. A comb is the E; turn it a quarter and it's the m. A circle is the O; cut it in half and it's the D.

Square, circle, half, square. The word has a rhythm you can draw from memory, and nothing in it is decoration.

It's the plainest way to say what the name says: one thing, put a different way.

Why it works

  • Reads MODE at a glance, even at 16 px.
  • Four heavy silhouettes: unmistakable on a small sachet, embossed or printed in one colour.
  • The construction explains itself in a sentence, which makes it easy to remember and repeat.

Watch out for

  • The m is a lowercase form among capitals. That's the charm, but set it with care beside other type.
  • Heavy marks need air: keep a clear space of half a letter all round.

Option 2 · after Lance Wyman's Mexico 68

Lines

MODE is never solid. Each letter is three parallel strokes that follow its skeleton and stop open at the ends: the M a triple zigzag, the O three rings, the E a triple bracket.

Lines like these vibrate. The word seems to hum, the way a good coffee hums along in the background of a morning.

It's the most expressive of the three: a logo that can run off the edge of a pack, or turn into a pattern.

Why it works

  • Instantly distinctive: nothing in coffee looks like it.
  • Becomes a pattern for free: extend the lines and the pack is covered.
  • Light and airy, so it feels fresh rather than heavy.

Watch out for

  • Fine lines close up below about 24 px. Use the single-line cut (shown below) for icons and tiny print.
  • Line logos depend on print quality; specify the minimum line weight with the printer.

Option 3 · after the negative space of Fletcher and Hollenstein

Counters

One black bar, four cuts. Only the counters (the holes inside letters) are cut out: a V for the M, a circle for the O, a half-circle for the D, two slots for the E.

You read the name from what's missing. It takes a beat, and that beat is the moment people remember.

On the sachet the bar becomes a band, like a seal across the pack. The ground colour shows through the counters.

Why it works

  • A real 'aha' logo: the kind people point out to each other.
  • A band works everywhere: across a sachet, as tape on a box, as a sticker.
  • Stencil-ready: it can be cut, punched, embossed or burned.

Watch out for

  • It reads a beat slower than the other two. Keep it large on first contact, and set the name in plain type nearby at first.
  • Always keep the whole bar. Cropping it loses the M or the E.

My pick

Two Shapes is the safest strong choice: it reads instantly and works everywhere. Counters is the one people will talk about. Lines is the most expressive, and the best if packaging and pattern matter most. Tell me which to take further.
