MODE · after Albert Hollenstein, L'Echoppe

Three marks from one small kit.

Hollenstein's L'Echoppe mark uses one square, two pieces of a circle and three hairlines, and it reads as a number, a letter and a picture at once. I took only its grammar, nothing else, and drew MODE three ways with it.

What I took from it

Design 1 · Quarters

Quarters

Hollenstein drew an e: a half-disc, a quarter-disc and one hairline under the bowl. I kept his e, built the other three letters from the same parts, and stacked them MO over DE, a compact block like his own mark.

Each letter is a coffee, and each still reads as its letter. The m is a square of coffee that drips: two arches bitten up to the halfway line, three legs ending in drops. The o is half poured from the side: solid on the right, a hairline on the left. The D is a coffee cup on its saucer: the straight back is the stem, the round belly is the bowl, the handle sits small and high.

The o and the e mirror each other: the o is solid on the right, the e on the left. The e's bowl is left open, with only the start of Hollenstein's hairline, so it can never close into a second o.

How it's built

At every size

At 32 px and below, the hairlines are drawn heavier.

Reversed

Why it works

  • It reads MODE in two short rows: the shapes are the letters, not a puzzle.
  • The coffee is in the letters, not added on: a drip, a pour, a cup and saucer.
  • Square overall, so it fits a sachet corner, a stamp, a lid or an app icon with no reworking.

Watch out for

  • The o's left half and the saucer are hairlines. At 32 px and below, use the small-size cut, which draws them heavier.
  • Always keep MO on top and DE below. It reads in rows; don't rearrange the four letters.

Design 2 · Hold & Finish

Hold & Finish

The reference has one rule at its heart: the solid shapes hold the letter, and a hairline finishes it. The 5 is a square and a line. The e is two solids and a line.

Here that rule is the whole logo. In every capital, the solid part holds the letter and a hairline completes it. M: the shoulders hold, the legs are lines. O: half solid, half line. D: a stem and a line. E: a stem and three lines.

It's a cup half poured, letter by letter, and a pack can be designed the same way: half solid, half line.

How it's built

At every size

At 32 px and below, the hairlines are drawn heavier.

Reversed

Why it works

  • One rule, applied four times: easy to explain, hard to forget.
  • Capitals give it authority on shelf. The hairlines keep it light rather than heavy.
  • The rule carries to the pack: fill half the sachet and leave the other half open.

Watch out for

  • The hairlines are the letters' second halves; if they disappear, it reads as four blocks. Use the heavier cut at 32 px and below.
  • Print the hairlines no thinner than 0.25 mm.

Design 3 · Pour

Pour

L'Echoppe has a cast: a square at top left, a half-disc, and hairline arcs that fly off it. It reads as 5é, and also as a little still life.

Pour keeps that cast and puts it to work. The square is the sachet. Its hairline arcs over and pours into the half-disc, which is the cup. A second hairline rises off the cup as steam, and a third is the handle.

Underneath, set the way the reference sets L'Echoppe: MODE in bold, Coffee in regular. Just the name and coffee.

How it's built

At every size

At 32 px and below, the hairlines are drawn heavier.

Reversed

Why it works

  • A real symbol: the whole product story in four shapes, from sachet to cup.
  • Works without the name: on the sachet's tear notch, as an app icon, on a cup sleeve.
  • The type is plain, so the symbol stays the hero.

Watch out for

  • It's a symbol plus a name, not a wordmark. Always lock up the two together at first.
  • The handle and steam are hairlines; use the heavier cut at 32 px and below.

My pick

Quarters is the one I'd take forward. It's the closest to the spirit of the reference: real letters made from pure parts, stacked into one block, with a drip, a pour and a cup hidden in them. Pour is the strongest symbol and the best if you want an icon that works without the name. Hold & Finish is the boldest on a shelf. Tell me which to develop.
